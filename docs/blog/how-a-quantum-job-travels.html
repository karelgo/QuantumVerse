A QuantumVerse explainer

How a quantum job travels

Follow one small circuit — two qubits, one entangling gate — from three lines of Python, down through a refrigerator colder than deep space, onto a chip the size of a fingernail, and back again as a column of numbers.

~13 min read end to end · user → qubit → user specimen: one Bell pair

Everyone can recite the poetry of quantum computing — superposition, entanglement, qubits that are somehow zero and one. Almost nobody shows the plumbing. When you actually "run something on a quantum computer," where does your code go? What physically touches the qubits? What comes back, and why is it a table of probabilities instead of an answer?

So let's trace one job the whole way down and back. Our specimen is the most famous two-qubit program there is — a Bell pair. Put one qubit into a superposition, entangle it with a second, measure both. If the machine is working, the two qubits will always agree: you get 00 or 11, roughly half and half, and essentially never 01 or 10. Three lines of code that, run on real hardware, set a small city of engineering in motion.

The twist you'll notice by the end: the physics at the bottom is exotic, but it's only a sliver of the trip. Most of a quantum computer is a very cold, very classical machine — and most of the wall-clock time isn't quantum at all.

# the entire specimen — a Bell pair in Qiskit from qiskit import QuantumCircuit qc = QuantumCircuit(2, 2) qc.h(0) # put qubit 0 into superposition qc.cx(0, 1) # entangle qubit 0 with qubit 1 qc.measure([0,1], [0,1]) result = backend.run(qc, shots=4096).result() # off it goes ↓
Stop 01 · Intent

You describe what, never how

The quantum computer never sees your Python. It sees a circuit: an ordered graph of gates acting on abstract qubits, with no notion of a real device, temperature, or wire. h(0) means "apply a Hadamard to logical qubit 0." It does not yet say which physical qubit that is, what pulse will implement it, or how long it takes. Like a tokenizer turning text into integers, the first thing that happens is a translation from human intent into a machine's native object — here, a device-independent list of operations.

This abstraction is the whole reason a program can outlive the machine it was written for. It's also exactly the layer a platform can standardize: our Bell pair, expressed in OpenQASM 3, is three instructions of portable text — the same circuit a hub could store, diff, and hand to any framework.

Stop 02 · Interchange

The circuit becomes portable text

Before it can travel, the circuit is serialized into an interchange format — OpenQASM 3 or QIR (a quantum extension of LLVM's intermediate representation). This is the quantum equivalent of bytecode: precise, framework-neutral, and small.

// the Bell pair, as OpenQASM 3 OPENQASM 3; include "stdgates.inc"; qubit[2] q; bit[2] c; h q[0]; cx q[0], q[1]; c = measure q;

Everything downstream — compilers, simulators, the hardware itself — speaks this language, not Qiskit's or Cirq's. It's a humble format that happens to be the linchpin of interoperability: if you can emit QASM, your work is portable; if you can't, it's trapped.

Stop 03 · The queue

Into the cloud, and into line

You almost never stand next to a quantum computer. You submit to one over the internet. The serialized job — circuit, number of shots (here 4,096), and options — is authenticated, wrapped, and dropped into a queue shared by everyone else on the planet aiming at that device. There are only a few dozen useful quantum computers in the world and a great many people who want them.

Here is the first counterintuitive truth: the queue usually dominates your wall-clock time. You may wait minutes or hours for a slot. The quantum part, when it finally runs, will be over in a few milliseconds. A quantum computer spends nearly all of its existence doing nothing quantum at all.

Stop 04 · Transpilation, part 1: layout

Choosing which real qubits to use

Your circuit talks about "qubit 0" and "qubit 1" as if all qubits were identical and fully connected. Real chips are neither. So a compiler — the transpiler — rewrites your circuit for one specific machine. Its first job is layout: deciding which physical qubits on the chip will play the roles of your logical ones.

This is not arbitrary. On a real device, every qubit has its own error rate, its own coherence, its own quality that shifts day to day. A good layout puts your circuit on the healthiest qubits that are also wired to each other. Pick badly and your Bell pair comes back mush; pick well and it sings — on the very same chip.

Stop 05 · Transpilation, part 2: routing

The tax of a sparse map

Two-qubit gates can only act on qubits that are physically connected. Our cx(0,1) needs qubits 0 and 1 to be neighbors. If the layout couldn't place them next to each other, the transpiler inserts SWAP gates to shuffle quantum states across the chip until the two partners are adjacent — like passing a note across a classroom hand to hand because you can't throw it directly.

SWAPs are expensive. Each one decomposes into three native two-qubit gates, and two-qubit gates are the noisiest, slowest operations you have. Qiskit uses a heuristic called SabreSwap to find a cheap-ish routing, because finding the optimal one is intractable. On a well-laid-out Bell pair the tax is zero. On a big circuit across a sparse chip, routing can multiply your gate count severalfold — pure overhead spent fighting the wiring diagram.

One SWAP costs three of your noisiest gates. Half of compiling a quantum program is just getting the right two qubits to stand next to each other.
Stop 06 · Transpilation, part 3: native gates

Down to the machine's real vocabulary

Your H and CX are conveniences. A given chip physically implements only a small native gate set — perhaps a couple of single-qubit rotations (like RZ and √X) plus one native entangler (an ECR or CZ). The transpiler rewrites every gate into that vocabulary using an equivalence library, then runs optimization passes to cancel redundant operations and shrink the circuit's depth.

You choose how hard it tries with an optimization level from 0 (map it and stop) to 3 (aggressively resynthesize blocks of gates into cheaper equivalents). Higher levels cost more compile time but produce shallower circuits that finish before noise catches up. Our humble Bell pair becomes a short, exact sequence of native pulses, ready for the metal.

Stop 07 · From gates to pulses

Every gate becomes a shaped microwave

A native gate is still an abstraction. To actually happen, it's compiled one level further — into pulses: precisely shaped analog waveforms, calibrated for this exact chip, that will nudge a qubit's state by just the right amount. A single-qubit gate is a microwave pulse a few tens of nanoseconds long, with a carefully sculpted envelope to avoid accidentally exciting the qubit out of its computational states.

These waveforms are synthesized by an arbitrary waveform generator (AWG) at a convenient intermediate frequency, then up-converted with an IQ mixer to the qubit's true operating frequency — around 5 GHz, squarely in the microwave band your phone's Wi-Fi also lives in. The abstract circuit is now, finally, a schedule of radio signals.

Stop 08 · Into the cold

A refrigerator colder than deep space

Those microwave signals now travel down into the strangest object in the building: a dilution refrigerator, the gold chandelier you've seen in photos. It cools in stages, each colder than the last, until the chip at the bottom sits at roughly 15 millikelvin — about 0.015°C above absolute zero, and colder than the 2.7 K of interstellar space. A superconducting qubit needs this because a whiff of heat is indistinguishable from a bit flip.

Going down, every control line is deliberately attenuated — roughly 60 dB in total, sprinkled across the stages — to strip away the thermal noise that room-temperature electronics radiate. You are, in effect, throwing away almost all of your signal on purpose, because what you throw away also carries the heat and noise that would drown the qubit. The wires are chosen for thermal cunning: lossy stainless steel on the way in, superconducting niobium-titanium on the delicate way out.

Stop 09 · The qubit

A fingernail of engineered atoms

At the bottom, finally, the quantum part: a chip smaller than a fingernail. On it, each transmon qubit is a tiny superconducting circuit — a loop of metal interrupted by a Josephson junction that behaves like a lossless, nonlinear inductor. Cooled into superconductivity, it becomes an artificial atom with cleanly separated energy levels. We christen the lowest two |0⟩ and |1⟩, and because the levels are unevenly spaced, we can talk to just those two without accidentally climbing higher.

This is the whole magic trick: an object big enough to wire up by hand, small and cold and quiet enough to behave by the rules of quantum mechanics. Everything above it in this article exists to protect and command this speck.

Stop 10 · Executing the gates

Nudges, and a race against forgetting

Now the schedule runs. To perform the Hadamard, a microwave pulse at the qubit's frequency drives it precisely halfway between |0⟩ and |1⟩ — a real, physical superposition. To perform the entangling gate, the control system uses a coupler between the two qubits so that the state of one becomes conditioned on the other. After these two operations the pair is entangled: no longer two separate qubits with their own values, but a single joint state that insists they'll be found to agree.

And all of it is a sprint. A qubit only holds its state for a coherence time — call it T1/T2 ≈ 200 microseconds — before noise erases it. Single-qubit gates take tens of nanoseconds, two-qubit gates a few hundred. Divide, and you get maybe a few thousand operations before the quantum information melts. That ratio, more than anything, is why today's machines are limited: not the number of qubits, but how many things you can do to them before they forget.

Stop 11 · Measurement

Reading a qubit without ever seeing it

You cannot look at a qubit directly. Instead, each one is coupled to its own tiny readout resonator, whose pitch shifts very slightly depending on whether the qubit is |0⟩ or |1⟩. To measure, the system sends a brief microwave probe tone at the resonator and listens to what bounces back: the qubit's state is written into the tiny phase and amplitude of the returning signal. This is dispersive readout — you never touch the qubit, you interrogate the room it echoes in.

That returning whisper is absurdly faint, so it climbs an amplification chain on the way out: a near-quantum-limited TWPA at the coldest stage, a HEMT amplifier at 4 K adding 30–40 dB, then room-temperature amplifiers adding another 30–40 dB. By the top it's a normal signal a digitizer can sample. Demodulated, each shot lands as a point in a 2-D I/Q plane, and the points fall into two clouds — one for 0, one for 1. A threshold between them turns physics back into a bit.

Stop 12 · Shots

One run tells you almost nothing

Here's the deepest break from classical computing. A single run of our Bell pair returns a single answer — say 11. Is that "the" answer? No. Measurement is probabilistic and it destroys the state; you get one bitstring, and the superposition is gone. The result of a quantum program isn't a value, it's a distribution, and you can only see a distribution by sampling it.

So the machine does the whole thing again. And again. It resets the qubits, replays the pulse schedule, measures, records the bitstring — 4,096 times for our job. Each cycle is a fresh, independent trial. Only when you stack them up does the physics appear: a histogram that piles up on 00 and 11 and leaves 01 and 10 nearly empty. That shape — not any single shot — is the answer.

You never read a qubit's value. You sample a probability distribution, thousands of times, and read its shape.
Stop 13 · The trip back up

Numbers climb out of the cold

Now everything runs in reverse, briefly. The tallied counts — how many 00s, how many 11s — are gathered by the control electronics, handed back to the provider's classical servers, and returned across the internet to the API you called. What went down as three lines of intent comes back as a small table of integers.

Often there's a cleanup step. Because readout and gates are imperfect, providers offer error mitigation: correcting for known readout biases with a calibration matrix, or techniques like zero-noise extrapolation that run the circuit at deliberately amplified noise and extrapolate back toward the ideal. (Note: mitigation is not error correction — today's machines are "noisy" by name. True fault tolerance is still being built.) The counts you receive may already be nudged toward the truth.

Stop 14 · What you get

A histogram, and an uncomfortable asterisk

Your result lands: roughly 49% 00, 49% 11, and a percent or two leaking into 01 and 10 that shouldn't be there at all. That leakage is the noise of the machine, made visible. The two qubits agreed, just as entanglement promised — most of the time.

And now the asterisk that defines this era. Run the identical job tomorrow, after the machine recalibrates overnight, and you'll get slightly different percentages. The qubits will have drifted; the error rates will have moved. Your result is inseparable from this device in this hour. That's what makes quantum results so hard to reproduce and compare — and why capturing the device's exact state alongside the numbers isn't bookkeeping, it's the only way the result means anything later. It's the problem QuantumVerse is built around.

Stop 15 · The road not taken

The same trip, on a different machine

We followed a superconducting machine, but that's a choice, not a law. Send the identical Bell pair to a trapped-ion computer and the middle of the story changes completely.

Detour: the trapped-ion route

There's no dilution refrigerator. The qubits are individual atoms, held in a vacuum at near-room-temperature by oscillating electric fields (a Paul trap) and cooled with lasers into a still, glinting crystal. Each qubit is a pair of electronic states inside a real atom — so every qubit is perfectly identical, a manufacturing luxury superconductors can only envy.

Gates are laser pulses, not microwaves. Single-qubit gates tap one ion; two-qubit gates are stranger and lovelier — a laser sets the ions rocking in a shared vibrational mode, and that collective wobble (its phonons) carries entanglement between them. Because any ion can share motion with any other, trapped ions enjoy all-to-all connectivity — no SWAP tax, no routing headache from Stop 05.

Readout is fluorescence: a laser asks each ion a question, and one state answers by glowing brightly while the other stays dark. A camera photographs which ions lit up. Bright is 1, dark is 0.

The trade: trapped-ion gates are far slower (microseconds to milliseconds) but reach the highest fidelities on record (>99.99%). Different physics, different strengths — identical circuit, identical distribution at the end. That's the quiet promise of the abstraction we started with.

Neutral-atom arrays, photonic chips, and spin qubits each rewrite the middle again while keeping the two ends — your circuit, your histogram — the same. The gates are universal even when the hardware isn't.

Most of a quantum computer is a refrigerator and a rack of radios. The quantum part is a cold speck you address with echoes and read by counting.

So that's the whole journey. Three lines of Python become portable text, wait in a queue, get rewritten for a specific chip's wiring and vocabulary, become shaped microwaves, plunge into the coldest place around, nudge a superconducting speck a few thousand times before it forgets, get read out through a five-stage amplifier as dots in a plane, and climb back up as a histogram stamped — invisibly — with the exact state of the machine that produced it.

Superposition and entanglement are the least of it. The engineering around the qubit is the real marvel — and the reason that sharing a circuit, running it anywhere, and trusting the number that comes back are three genuinely hard, genuinely unsolved problems. Which is the point of everything we're building.
